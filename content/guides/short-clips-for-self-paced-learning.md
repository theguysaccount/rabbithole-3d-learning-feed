Long lecture recordings are easy to publish and hard to learn from: learners can't see the structure, can't skip what they know, and can't find the five minutes they need. Short clips fix the first two problems only if they're connected well.

## Cut by question, not by time

A good clip answers one question someone might actually ask: "what is a basis?", "why does the loss go down?". Cutting every ten minutes produces clips that start and end mid-thought. Go through the recording, write the question each stretch answers, and cut at those boundaries. With start and end times you can do this without editing the file.

## Give every clip a reason to exist

Write a one-line note for each clip saying what it answers. If you can't, the clip probably belongs inside the one before or after it.

## Connect them as a path, not a pile

A folder of thirty clips is a pile. A path has an order and a depth:

- **Order:** the main track is the sequence that builds understanding.
- **Depth:** clips that only some learners need sit under the clip that raises the need.

## Let learners see where they are

A map of the path, with what they've already watched marked, lets learners skip ahead confidently and come back to what they skipped. Rabbit Hole's player keeps that map next to the video.
